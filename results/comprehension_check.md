# Comprehension check: three answers to read aloud

Read each situation and answer to someone outside the team. Then ask: *"What would you do now?"* Write down their words and whether they got it. Results go in `docs/results.md`, section 6.

## A handoff to a person · DSA-D-0003

**The person wrote:** Someone called from 'my operator' offering a free SIM upgrade. I got a code by text and he asked me to read it out. Was that really you?

**The assistant asked first:** Did you read out or forward the code to the caller?

**The assistant answered:** If you read out or forwarded the code, contact the operator now so it can stop the eSIM transfer and lock your number. I am connecting you now to an operator support agent. That call was not from us. A code like this can move your number to another device, and banks send their codes to it. If you did not read the code out, do not share it and ignore it. Then nothing more is needed beyond keeping your number locked.

1. Let me connect you to an operator support agent now.
2. Ask the operator to lock your number and stop the eSIM transfer.
3. Never share codes or PINs, even with someone who says they are from your operator.
4. Then contact your bank, because banks send their codes to this number.

## A harmless case · DSA-D-0026

**The person wrote:** HELP! I received an email about my bill but I'm not sure it's real. There's no link in it.

**The assistant answered:** Nothing needs to be done about this email. It looks like a genuine bill notice. It has no links, asks for no payment details or codes, and just says the bill is in your online account. If you want to double-check, open the operator's app yourself and look at the bill there. An old forum leak that exposed only an email address is not a reason to worry here.

1. Nothing needs to be done.
2. Check the November bill in the operator's own app, opened yourself.

## A small-business case · DSA-D-0005

**The person wrote:** Asking on behalf of our company. My employee signed in with their work email on a document sharing page that turned out to be a scam. What do we need to do?

**The assistant answered:** Have your employee change their work email password now, from a device they trust. The page was a login scam, so treat the password as known to the scammer. Also sign out all their sessions and turn on two-step login. If the same password is used elsewhere, change it there too.

1. Change the employee's work email password now from a trusted device.
2. Sign out all of their sessions and review recent logins.
3. Turn on two-step login for their email.
4. Check that no forwarding rules have been added to the mailbox.
